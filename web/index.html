How the kinematics works

The arm has one slide and three joints.
The slide runs along the rail. That is X.
All three joints turn the same way. So the arm always stays flat, in one plane.
That means the maths here is 2D. Just triangles.
Every number below is taken from the same code the PLC runs. This page does no maths of its own.

Frames & their zero points

These numbers are live. Move the robot and watch them change.
what a "zero" means here
Zero is where measuring starts from.
World zero: on the floor, middle of the rail.
Joint zero: all angles at 0. The arm points straight out.
Tool zero (TCP): the tip of the fingers. That is the point the robot really moves.
In the 3D view: red = X, green = Y, blue = Z.

Forward: angles → position

Angles in, position out.
Every link is a stick of known length, turned by its own angle.
cos gives the sideways part. sin gives the upward part.
Add the parts up: that is the wrist. Add the tool: that is the fingers.

Inverse: position → angles

Position in, angles out. The other way round.
1. Take the tool and the last link off the target. What is left is the wrist.
2. Measure R: the straight line from shoulder to wrist.
3. Now you have a triangle. Sides L2, L3 and R — all three known.
Three known sides means the angles are known too (law of cosines). That is the whole trick. The yellow triangle in the 3D view is this one.
If R is longer than L2 + L3, the arm cannot reach. No triangle, no answer.
source
why two answers exist
The triangle can fold two ways: elbow down, or elbow up.
Both put the fingers in the same spot.
The real machine uses elbow down, so this does too. The checkbox in the control panel tries the mirror.

Round trip

Put the answer back into the forward formula.
If the target comes back out, both directions agree.
why it never reaches zero
The PLC's constants are cut to 9 digits (DEGREE_TO_RAD = 0.017453292).
Degrees → radians → degrees loses a tiny bit each time.
About 0.000005° is left over. That is the floor, not a bug. Using the exact same constants as the PLC is what matters here.

how cycle time is measured
Counted out to out: from one board leaving WIP OUT to the next one leaving. That is what decides boards per hour.
The first board is not counted — there is nothing before it, and the time to fill an empty cell is not a cycle time.
The clock runs only while the cycle runs. A stop is not part of the cell's capability.
avg 10 is divided by how many samples exist, not always by 10 — otherwise the first minutes read far better than the truth.

why the buttons behave like this
Changing the selector while running, E-STOP, or a collision stops the robot where it is and demands Home before Autorun will run again: after a sudden stop the sequencer does not know what pose the axes ended in. Cycle stop is different — the job in hand is finished first, so no board is left in mid-air, and Autorun can be used again straight away. A board being gripped is kept held through all of that, and Autorun resumes its delivery. Home folds the arm only — the rail stays put, so home is valid anywhere along it. Speed scales axis velocity (cycle and jog), not acceleration and not the gripper; clamped to 1..100 % in the PLC.

Stations

buffer rule
Empty ICCs are filled first, both of them → a finished DW is cleared to WIP OUT → a finished ICC moves into a free DW. That order is what keeps the 17-second testers busy, and what stops four full machines from deadlocking each other. Every machine times itself, concurrently.

jog notes
The real machine jogs one step at a time (10); "hold to move" is an addition for this view. Jog is live only with the selector at MANUAL — two command sources for one arm fight every scan. Opening the gripper while holding a board DROPS it, and clears the job with it. A pose that would enter a machine body is rejected by the PLC before any axis moves. Elbow up exists only in the V2 FB; off = the same branch the machine uses.
